# Career guidance, psychology and learning resources

## Holland RIASEC interest types
Career psychology model with six interest types: Realistic (hands-on, tools), Investigative (analysis, research), Artistic (creative expression), Social (helping, teaching), Enterprising (leading, persuading, selling), Conventional (organising, detail, systems). Most people are a blend of two or three. Mapping: Investigative and Conventional fit data and engineering roles; Enterprising with Investigative fits product management and founding; Artistic with Investigative fits design; Social with Investigative fits developer advocacy, teaching and customer success. Use it to ask what activities energise someone, not to box them in.

## Ikigai: finding the overlap
A framework with four questions: what you love, what you are good at, what the world needs, and what you can be paid for. A good career option sits in the overlap of at least three. When a student is unsure, list activities they lose track of time doing, then test each against the other three questions. Treat it as a conversation guide, not a formula.

## Taking stock of skills, strengths and weaknesses
Separate three things: skills you have, skills you enjoy using, and skills the market pays for. Strengths are things you do well and find energising. Weaknesses are either fixable gaps, which become a learning plan, or things you dislike, which should shape what you avoid. Ask about concrete examples such as a project they were proud of or a task they procrastinate on, rather than abstract traits.

## Choosing a path when you are unsure
Run small cheap experiments instead of deciding in theory. Talk to three people in a role, shadow or do a small project, take a short course, and notice what energises or drains you. Decide for the next one to two years, not for life; careers are rarely linear. Prefer options that keep doors open, such as roles that build transferable skills like data literacy, communication and shipping real work.

## Computer science student who dislikes heavy coding
Many technology careers use technical understanding without heads-down coding all day. Strong options: product manager, business analyst, technical program manager, solutions engineer, UX or product designer, developer advocate, technical writer, customer success engineer, data analyst, and consulting. Suggested approach: pick the two that match interests, build one proof of work for each, and keep core technical literacy such as SQL and how APIs work because it differentiates these candidates.

## Switching careers or moving between roles
Identify transferable skills, close the gap with one targeted project rather than many courses, and reframe past experience in the language of the new role. The easiest switches are adjacent: engineer to solutions engineer or product manager, analyst to product or data science, support to QA or engineering. Expect a possible short-term step sideways to enter a new track; plan for it and keep it to under a year.

## Building a portfolio and proof of work
Employers hire evidence. Build two or three projects that solve real problems, deploy them at a live link, write a short case study for each covering the problem, your decisions, results and what you would improve. Contribute to open source or volunteer for a nonprofit to get real users. A clean public profile on GitHub or LinkedIn with these projects beats a long list of certificates.

## Internships, campus placements and getting the first job
In India, campus placements, off-campus applications, referrals and internships are the main entry points. Start internships by the second or third year. Apply early, tailor your resume to the role with measurable outcomes, and use referrals by building relationships with alumni. For core computer science roles prepare data structures, algorithms and a project you can explain deeply; for product, analytics and consulting prepare case questions and metrics thinking.

## Further study: master's, MBA, GATE and certifications
A master's or MS abroad fits research, deep machine learning or specialised fields. An MBA fits management, consulting, product leadership or finance and usually works best after 2 to 4 years of work experience. GATE leads to M.Tech admissions and public sector roles. Certifications such as cloud associate exams or data analytics certificates help as signals for specific entry roles but do not replace projects. Decide based on the role you want, the cost, and whether the credential is truly required for it.

## Interview preparation basics
Prepare a two minute story about yourself linking background, projects and why this role. For behavioural questions use situation, action, result with numbers. For technical roles practise explaining your projects and design choices out loud. For product roles practise structuring problems, defining success metrics and prioritising. Practising aloud with a friend or a mock interview improves delivery far more than silent reading.

## Communication, soft skills and career resilience
Across roles, strong writing, clear speaking, collaboration and the ability to learn quickly are consistent differentiators and become more important at senior levels. Build them by presenting project work, writing short posts or documents, and seeking feedback. Rejection and slow progress are normal; track small wins, keep a learning routine and build a network of peers and mentors.

## Learning resources and learning paths
Free and low cost paths: CS50 and freeCodeCamp for programming foundations; Mode SQL tutorials and StrataScratch for SQL; Khan Academy for statistics; Coursera machine learning courses and fast dot ai for ML; Google UX and Product Management certificates for entry level design and product; Figma community files for design practice; Lenny's Newsletter and Reforge articles for product; roadmap dot sh for step by step skill maps by role. Choose one path, finish a project from it, and then move on.
